Every high school student should complete a personal finance course before graduation — not as a luxury elective, but as a basic requirement, the same way we treat algebra or civics. Adults are expected to choose loans, health plans, tax withholdings, and retirement accounts with almost no formal preparation. We then act surprised when people carry expensive credit-card balances, skip emergency savings, or sign papers they do not understand. That gap is not a private failing. It is a curriculum failure.

A required course would not turn seventeen-year-olds into hedge-fund managers. It would teach a short list of durable skills: how interest compounds for and against you; how to read a pay stub; what a credit report is and how it is used; the difference between a grant, a subsidized loan, and a private student loan; how insurance deductibles work; and why “buy now, pay later” is still debt. Those topics show up in the first five years after graduation whether a student goes to college, a trade program, the military, or a job. Leaving them to family dinner-table wisdom guarantees that the students with the least financial cushion get the least guidance.

The usual counterargument is that schools are already overcrowded with mandates, and that families or employers should teach money. That sounds reasonable until you look at who actually receives that teaching. Parents who are themselves anxious about debt, working two jobs, or excluded from mainstream banking are in a poor position to offer a syllabus. Employers teach you how to use their payroll portal, not how to decide whether a 401(k) match is worth more than paying off a high-interest card. A school course is the one intervention that reaches nearly every student, including those whose families cannot.

None of this requires turning teachers into licensed advisors or picking political fights about markets. A good course is practical and humble. It uses real forms, real sample APRs, and honest talk about scams. It can sit in a semester, taught by existing social-studies or business faculty with a shared curriculum, the way health class already covers topics families also “should” handle at home.

We already accept that literacy includes more than decoding words. Financial literacy is part of that same civic minimum. If we graduate students who can analyze a novel but cannot parse a loan disclosure, we have prepared them for a world that no longer exists. Require the course. Measure it. Improve it. The cost of a semester is small next to the cost of a generation learning interest the hard way.
