# A Class Every Senior Should Take

There is a class that should be required of every high school student in the country, and it is not calculus. It is personal finance — one semester, before graduation, no exceptions. The case for it is so plain that the real puzzle is why we are still arguing about it.

Most Americans will not use calculus after high school. All of them will use money. They will sign leases, take out car loans, carry credit-card balances, choose health insurance, decide whether to start a 401(k), and — for many — buy a house they cannot really afford. These are not optional skills. They are the basic machinery of adult life, and getting them wrong is expensive in a way that compounds. A 22-year-old who carries a $2,000 credit-card balance at 22% interest, making minimum payments, will spend years and thousands of dollars paying for what was, in effect, a few weeks of dinner out. A 25-year-old who starts investing $200 a month in a low-cost index fund, instead of waiting until 35 to "have enough," retires with hundreds of thousands more. These are not edge cases. They are the median story.

The standard counterargument is that personal finance is the parents' job, and that schools should stick to academics. It is a kind argument and a wrong one. Plenty of parents are themselves financially fragile, and children inherit their mistakes along with their habits. A class does not replace a parent; it gives the child a fair chance to do better than the household they grew up in, which is supposed to be the point of public education. The same logic that has us teach reading — because not every home teaches it — applies here.

There is also a civic argument. A country in which most adults cannot read a loan contract or estimate compound interest is a country in which predatory lending thrives and retirement becomes a public problem rather than a private plan. We pay, one way or another, for financial illiteracy — in bankruptcies, in foreclosures, in neighbors who work until seventy-five because they cannot afford not to. Teaching the subject in school is cheaper than the alternative, and it is fairer.

A good personal finance class is not hard to design. Budgeting, taxes, credit, compound interest, the basics of investing, the basics of insurance, and how to read a pay stub will do. None of it is beyond a seventeen-year-old. What is beyond a seventeen-year-old is figuring it out alone, in the middle of a first job, with a credit card offer already in the mail. We should stop asking them to.
