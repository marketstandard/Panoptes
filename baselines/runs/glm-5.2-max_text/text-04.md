The fog had been thick for three days, the kind that turned the beam into a soft column instead of a knife, so Walt almost missed it the first night. A blink, down the coast, where no blink had any business being. He set his coffee down and leaned into the glass.

Tillamook Light stood dark for thirty years. The Coast Guard had decommissioned it in '94, stripped the lens, welded the door, and let the gulls take the rest. Everybody along this stretch of Highway 101 knew that. So when a steady one-on, one-off pulse began to show through the murk at roughly the right bearing, Walt did what keepers do: he checked his watch, then his eyes, then his watch again.

The pattern was too regular for a boat and too slow for an aircraft beacon. Three seconds on, three off. He counted a full minute. It did not drift.

He picked up the handset to call the station in Newport, then put it back. There was no good way to say it. He pulled the logbook across the desk instead and wrote the time, the bearing, and the period, and under "remarks" he put a single question mark, the way his predecessor had taught him forty years back when something didn't fit and the sea was too loud to argue with.

By dawn the fog had thinned and the light was gone. Walt climbed down to the rocks anyway and found, just above the tide line, a single boot print in the sand that had not been there the night before.
