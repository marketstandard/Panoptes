We send millions of teenagers out into the world each year armed with an understanding of calculus, the intricacies of the American Revolution, and the periodic table of elements. Yet, astoundingly, a significant portion of these young adults graduate high school without knowing how to balance a budget, understand interest rates, or file their taxes. In an increasingly complex economic landscape, this educational blind spot is a profound disservice to our youth. It is time to mandate that every high school student complete a comprehensive personal finance course before receiving their diploma.

The financial decisions young adults make in their late teens and early twenties can dictate their economic trajectory for decades. Upon turning eighteen, students are bombarded with credit card offers and complex student loan agreements. Without a foundational understanding of compound interest, a young person can easily accumulate thousands of dollars in high-interest debt before they even secure their first full-time job. A mandatory personal finance curriculum would equip students with the practical tools they need to navigate adulthood. They would learn how to build an emergency fund, understand the distinction between good and bad debt, compare insurance policies, and grasp the vital importance of starting to save for retirement early. 

A common counterargument against making personal finance a graduation requirement is that the high school curriculum is already overcrowded. Critics argue that adding another mandatory class would force schools to cut valuable electives or arts programs, and that financial literacy is a responsibility best left to parents. 

While parental guidance is invaluable, relying solely on families to teach personal finance perpetuates systemic inequality. Students whose parents struggle with debt or lack financial literacy themselves are structurally disadvantaged, creating a generational cycle of financial instability. The public education system exists to provide a baseline of essential knowledge to all citizens, regardless of their background. By integrating personal finance into the core curriculum, we level the playing field, ensuring that every student has access to the strategies required for economic self-sufficiency. 

Teaching teenagers how to manage money is not an extracurricular luxury; it is a critical survival skill in the modern world. By making personal finance a graduation requirement, we can empower the next generation to make informed decisions, avoid crippling debt, and build a foundation for long-term financial health. We owe it to our students to teach them not just how to make a living, but how to manage one.
