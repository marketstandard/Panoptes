Composting turns kitchen scraps and yard waste into a dark, crumbly soil amendment called humus, and it depends on balancing nitrogen-rich "greens" with carbon-rich "browns," commonly at about three parts browns to one part greens by volume. Microorganisms do most of the work and need moisture and oxygen, so the pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge and be turned every week or two — a hot pile can finish in two to three months, while a neglected cold pile may take a year. Meat, dairy, and oily foods are usually excluded from open piles because they attract pests, though enclosed systems can handle them, and finished compost smells earthy rather than sour with none of the original material still recognizable.
