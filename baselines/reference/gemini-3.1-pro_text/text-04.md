The storm had been battering the Oregon coast for three days, tossing sheets of freezing rain against the reinforced glass of the lantern room. Elias Thorne stood at the precipice of the Tillamook Head lighthouse, nursing a mug of coffee that had gone cold an hour ago. Out here, isolation was a heavy, physical thing, broken only by the rhythmic, mechanical sweeping of his own beacon cutting through the fog. 

He raised his binoculars, a habitual gesture born of thirty years of scanning the empty, churning expanse of the Pacific. To the north, just barely visible through the shifting mist, stood the jagged silhouette of the Blackwood Shoals lighthouse. It had been decommissioned in 1996, its spiraling iron staircase left to rust and its great Fresnel lens plunged into permanent darkness after the tragic disappearance of its last keeper. 

Elias lowered the binoculars, blinked, and raised them again. 

A sharp prickle of unease crawled up the back of his neck. Through the dense gray squall, a pale, anemic light pierced the gloom. It wasn’t a reflection, and it wasn’t the moon. It was a deliberate, piercing yellow beam. 

Flash. Two seconds of darkness. 
Flash. Flash. Four seconds of darkness. 
Flash. 

Elias’s breath hitched, fogging the cold glass in front of him. He knew the old Blackwood signal code by heart. It wasn't just a random electrical short or a trick of the storm. Someone was over there, across three miles of deadly, treacherous water, and they were manually operating the lamp to send a message. A message that hadn't been used since the night the last keeper vanished.
