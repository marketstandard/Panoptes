# Teach Them the Math That Will Actually Bill Them

We require American teenagers to dissect a fetal pig, memorize the quadratic formula, and identify the causes of the War of 1812. We do not require them to understand what happens when they carry a balance on a credit card at 24% APR. Then we hand them a diploma, and within a few years many of them sign the largest financial contract of their lives — a student loan, a car note, a lease — with no formal preparation whatsoever.

Every high school student should complete a personal finance course before graduation.

The case is not abstract. The average new-car loan in this country now stretches past six years, and a meaningful share of borrowers are underwater the moment they drive off the lot. Credit card debt sits at record highs. Roughly half of American adults report they could not cover an unexpected four-figure expense without borrowing. These are not failures of intelligence. They are failures of instruction. Compound interest is not intuitive — it is a mathematical process that runs quietly in the background and rewards only the people who were told it exists.

A semester course can cover the essentials: how interest accrues in both directions, how to read a pay stub and understand withholding, what a credit score measures and what actually moves it, how to compare loan offers, what an index fund is and why fees matter over forty years, and how insurance transfers risk. None of this is difficult. It is simply never taught.

The most common objection is that parents should handle this, not schools. It is an appealing argument and a self-defeating one. Financial literacy is not evenly distributed among parents — it correlates strongly with income, education, and wealth. Leaving it to families guarantees that the students who most need the knowledge are least likely to receive it, and the advantage compounds across generations exactly the way the interest does. We do not leave literacy or civics to parental discretion for the same reason. When a subject is both essential and unequally available at home, that is the strongest possible argument for teaching it in school, not the weakest.

A second objection — that the curriculum is already full — deserves a straight answer: yes, and so we should choose. A semester of personal finance will affect more graduates' lives, more often, than a semester of nearly anything else competing for the slot.

We teach students how to analyze a poem they will never read again. We can teach them how to read a loan agreement they will sign next year.
