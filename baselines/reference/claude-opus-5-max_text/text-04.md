The light came back on a little after two in the morning.

Halvard Ness saw it from the gallery of Cape Ivy Light, where he had gone to smoke the one cigarette a day he still allowed himself, and for a moment he simply watched it the way you watch a bird you cannot name. Four miles north, across the black notch of water where the coast bent inward, Trelawny Rock had stood dark for thirty years. Dark since before his hair went white. Dark since the Coast Guard pulled the last keeper off it in 1971 and welded the door.

Now it was blinking.

He counted, because counting was what he did. Two seconds on. Four off. Two on. Four off. Steady as a metronome, patient as a man with nowhere to be.

The cigarette burned down to his knuckle and he did not notice.

An automated beacon would have been white and fast and thoughtless. This light was warm — oil-warm, the color of the old fourth-order Fresnel he'd trained on as a boy — and it had a hesitation in it, a hitch on the fourth repetition, as though whoever was working the shutter had to shift his weight.

Halvard went inside. The radio gave him nothing but the long hiss of a dead channel. He tried the Coast Guard station at Yaquina and got a young man who said, kindly, in the voice people used with him now, that Trelawny had been decommissioned before he was born, sir, and that fog does strange things to a light.

There was no fog.

He stood at the window until dawn came up gray behind the headland and washed the whole coast flat and ordinary again. The light went out at 5:41.

The next night, he was waiting with a notebook.
