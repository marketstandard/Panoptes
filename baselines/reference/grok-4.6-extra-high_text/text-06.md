Composting turns kitchen scraps and yard waste into a dark, crumbly soil amendment called humus, using a balance of nitrogen-supplying “greens” such as vegetable peels and fresh grass clippings and carbon-supplying “browns” such as dried leaves and cardboard, with a widely cited rule of roughly three parts browns to one part greens by volume. Microorganisms do most of the work and need moisture and oxygen, so a pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge and be turned every week or two, and under good conditions a hot pile can finish in two to three months while a neglected cold pile may take a year. Meat, dairy, and oily foods are usually excluded from open piles because they attract pests, though enclosed systems can handle them, and finished compost smells earthy rather than sour with none of the original material recognizable.
