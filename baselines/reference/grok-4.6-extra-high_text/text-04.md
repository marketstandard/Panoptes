Mara Ellison had kept the Cape Arago light for eleven years, long enough that the Pacific’s weather had become a kind of clock. Fog meant the horn. Clear nights meant the slow, familiar sweep of her own lamp across black water, and, to the south, a darker tooth of rock where the old Tillow Head lighthouse stood empty.

Tillow Head had been unmanned for thirty years. The Coast Guard had decommissioned it, boarded the lantern room, and left the tower to gulls and rust. Mara used it the way other keepers used a mountain: a fixed point. Dark meant the world was behaving.

Just after two, she looked up from the log and saw a white pulse on that southern tooth. Not a reflection. Not a boat. A blink, then dark, then a blink again, as regular as a metronome.

She counted without meaning to. Four seconds on. Four off. Four on. Four off.

The radio gave her only static and a weather loop. She stepped onto the gallery, binoculars cold in her hands. The beam was coming from the lantern room itself — from behind boards that should have been nailed shut. It did not sweep. It stared, then vanished, then stared.

Mara wrote the times in the log with a hand that wanted to shake and refused. She told herself it was kids, a generator, some prank with a work light. She told herself that until the pattern broke.

Three short. Three long. Three short.

Then the four-second pulse resumed, as if the tower had only cleared its throat.

She reached for the phone. The line was dead. Below, on the path down to the cove, a single lantern was moving toward her door.
