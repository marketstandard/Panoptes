The light was wrong.

Marta Ellery had kept the lamp at Heceta Head for eleven years, and in all that time the horizon south of her tower had been dark. Alcatraz Light — everyone still called it that, though the rock's true name was something older — had gone cold in 1996, when the Coast Guard automated the last of the Oregon stations and left the old tower to the cormorants. She knew its silhouette the way she knew the scar on her own hand: the squat column, the missing lantern room, the gull-whitened dome.

Now it was blinking.

She stood at the gallery rail with her coffee going cold, counting. Three seconds on. Six off. Three on. Six off. Not the random flicker of a campfire or some squatter's lantern. A pattern. The same pattern, she realized with a small chill, that Alcatraz had burned before the automation crews came — the signature every mariner within forty miles had once steered by.

She logged it at 03:12, because that was the job, and then she did something the manual had never covered: she drove the nine miles of gravel road to the headland at dawn.

The tower door was chained and rusted, exactly as the survey photos showed it. But the padlock hung open. And on the threshold, arranged with terrible patience, stood a row of thirty glass prisms — one, she would understand later, for every year the light had slept.
