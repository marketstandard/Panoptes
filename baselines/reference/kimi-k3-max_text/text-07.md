Before They Swipe: The Case for Mandatory Personal Finance

Every spring, millions of American teenagers walk across a graduation stage able to recite the Pythagorean theorem but unable to read a credit card statement. Within a year, many of them will sign student loan documents, open their first credit cards, and make financial decisions with consequences that stretch decades into the future. We send them into this arena unarmed. Every high school student should be required to complete a personal finance course before graduation, and the case is stronger than ever.

The numbers are not subtle. Total U.S. credit card debt has passed a trillion dollars, carried largely by people who were never taught how compound interest works against them. Young adults now navigate buy-now-pay-later schemes, variable-rate loans, and retirement plans that assume a level of financial literacy our schools never provided. A semester of personal finance — budgeting, interest, taxes, insurance, investing basics — costs little and pays back for a lifetime. Studies of states with financial literacy mandates consistently find that students who take these courses carry less high-interest debt and hold better credit scores in early adulthood.

The most common counterargument is that personal finance belongs at home — that parents, not schools, should teach money skills. It sounds reasonable until you examine it. Parents can only teach what they know, and millions of adults are themselves struggling: living paycheck to paycheck, carrying revolving debt, unsure how a 401(k) works. Making home the sole source of financial education doesn't return responsibility to families; it guarantees that financial literacy remains inherited privilege, flowing to the children of the financially secure and skipping everyone else. School is the one institution that reaches every student, which is precisely why it must carry this load.

Others object that the curriculum is already crowded. But we require years of mathematics and then withhold the single course where that math touches a student's actual life. If we can mandate gym class, we can mandate the class that determines whether a twenty-two-year-old understands her loan terms.

Graduation is supposed to certify readiness for adult life. Until a diploma includes the ability to budget, borrow wisely, and save, it certifies something less. Teach the money course. Require it. Our students' futures are worth one semester.
