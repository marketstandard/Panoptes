We make eighteen-year-olds sign loan papers that can follow them to the grave. We just don't teach them what the papers say.

Every spring, millions of high school seniors commit to student loans, car loans, and their first credit cards, and most of them could not tell you the difference between a subsidized and unsubsidized loan if their credit score depended on it — which, by the way, it does. We require algebra, which most adults never use, and treat compound interest, which every adult lives with, as an elective. That's backwards, and every high school student should have to pass a personal finance course before graduation.

The case isn't complicated. Money mistakes made at eighteen are uniquely expensive. A missed payment at nineteen can haunt a rental application at twenty-five. A maxed-out card with a 29% APR doesn't feel like a trap when the minimum payment is $35, and that's exactly why it's a trap. Meanwhile the entire consumer economy is engineered to separate inexperienced people from their future earnings — buy-now-pay-later at checkout, overdraft "courtesy" fees, financing plans designed to be unreadable. We send kids into that arena unarmed and then blame them for getting hurt.

A good course isn't abstract. It teaches kids to read a pay stub, build a budget that survives contact with reality, understand what a credit score actually measures, and run the real math on a car loan versus the sticker price. It explains retirement accounts to people for whom retirement is forty years away, because forty years is precisely why starting early matters more than starting big.

The common counterargument is that this is a parent's job. Sure — in a country where every parent understood this stuff and had time to teach it. In the actual country, nearly two-thirds of adults live paycheck to paycheck, and plenty of parents are passing down their own bad habits or silence. We don't say "fractions are a parent's job." We teach fractions because a society needs numerate citizens. It needs financially literate ones at least as much.

The other objection is curriculum crowding: something has to give. Fine. Let it. If we can find a semester for cursive — and most schools did, for a century — we can find one for the subject that determines whether a graduate can keep an apartment.

Graduation should mean ready. Right now it doesn't. A required personal finance course won't fix wages or rent, but it would stop us from handing teenagers a loaded economy with no safety briefing. That's the least a diploma should guarantee.
