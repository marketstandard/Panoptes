The light was wrong.

Eli Vance had watched the water off Cape Perpetua for nineteen years, and he knew every light on this stretch of coast the way a shepherd knows sheep. His own lamp, sweeping its slow white arc. The buoy at the reef, red, four seconds. The fishing boats that came and went like fireflies with engines.

And then there was Heceta Point — dark since '96, unmanned, a concrete skeleton the tourists photographed at sunset. The Coast Guard had stripped the lens when they automated everything else. Eli had watched them haul it away on a barge. He'd waved, actually, like an idiot, at a funeral for a light.

So when it blinked at 2:14 in the morning, Eli put down his coffee and counted.

One. Two. Three. Dark for six. One. Two. Three. Dark for six.

Not a reflection. Not a boat. The pattern came from the tower itself, steady as a metronome, and it had a color he couldn't place — not the warm white of the old Fresnel lamps, something greener, like light through deep water.

He radioed the station at Newport. The kid on duty humored him for about a minute. "Heceta's decommissioned, Mr. Vance. There's nothing up there to blink. Probably campers with a spotlight." Campers. In October. In a tower with no door, because the door had been welded shut before Eli's daughter was born.

The light blinked again. Three short, six dark. Three short, six dark. Eli found himself reaching for the logbook, the real one, the leather one his predecessor had kept from 1961. He flipped to the back, to the pages nobody was supposed to write in anymore, and started recording times.

It was only when his hand stopped moving that he realized why the pattern felt familiar. Three short. Six dark. His predecessor had logged the same rhythm in October of 1974, three nights before the Marlowe went down with all hands.

The entry ended mid-sentence. Eli had read it a hundred times.

Tonight, for the first time, he understood it.
