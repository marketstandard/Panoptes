1. **Scrub it clean.** Wash the skillet with hot water and a stiff brush — a little soap is fine for a first cleaning, despite what your uncle says. Dry it completely, then set it on a burner for a minute to chase off any hidden moisture.

2. **Oil it thin.** Rub a teaspoon of neutral oil (canola or grapeseed work great) over every surface — inside, outside, handle. Then take a clean rag and wipe it like you regret it. The layer left behind is what you want; too much oil turns sticky.

3. **Bake it upside down.** Put the pan in a 450°F oven, upside down, with a sheet of foil on the rack below to catch drips. Bake for one hour. The oil polymerizes — it bonds to the iron and turns into that hard, slick coating.

4. **Repeat for a real foundation.** One round of seasoning is a start, not a finish. Do the oil-and-bake cycle two or three more times for a new or stripped pan. You'll see it darken from gray to a deep brown-black.

5. **Cook fatty foods early.** For the first few weeks, favor bacon, cornbread, fried anything. Acidic stuff like tomato sauce can strip young seasoning, so save the marinara for later.

6. **Clean it gently, dry it always.** After cooking, wipe the pan out, or scrub with salt and a little water for stuck bits. Never soak it, never leave it wet — rust is the only real enemy here. Dry on the stove, then wipe on a whisper of oil.

7. **Fix mistakes without panic.** Food sticking or a little rust? Scrub it down with steel wool, re-oil, and re-bake. Cast iron is nearly indestructible; almost nothing is unrecoverable, which is the whole point of owning it.
