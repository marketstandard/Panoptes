Panoptes: An Evidence Observatory for Calibrated AI-Text Attribution with Verifiable Community Baselines

Encryptic1 and the Panoptes Contributors
Independent open research software project — github.com/marketstandard/Panoptes
August 12, 2026 · Working paper, v1.0

Abstract

Automated judgments about whether a document was written or assisted by a large language model are increasingly consequential, yet the dominant tooling presents a single opaque score with little account of calibration, sample size, or the conditions under which the judgment fails. We present Panoptes, an open, local-first system that treats AI-text attribution as an evidence problem rather than a classification problem. Panoptes combines a calibrated Bayesian posterior over participation classes, one-sided binomial hypothesis tests for known public watermark schemes with false-discovery-rate control and explicit power analysis, and signed provenance verification, surfacing every quantity with its assumptions and limits. Complementing the analyzer, we introduce a verifiable community baseline protocol: a canonical prompt set, hash-pinned run manifests with Merkle commitments, and an append-only public catalog that records cryptographic evidence of a run without collecting raw model outputs, optionally anchored to the Bitcoin blockchain via OpenTimestamps. We describe the architecture, the statistical contracts, the threat model, and twelve reference runs across six frontier models, and we discuss the fundamental limits of text-only attribution.

Keywords — AI-text detection · watermark hypothesis testing · calibration · provenance · reproducible baselines · hash-chained evidence catalogs

1. Introduction

The question “was this text written by a machine?” is now asked daily by educators, editors, reviewers, and platforms. The tools typically answering it reduce a document to a single number, offered without a statement of the evidence that produced it, the sample size behind it, or the regimes in which it should not be trusted. False positives carry real costs for the accused; false negatives carry real costs for institutions. A defensible tool must therefore do more than score: it must show its evidence, keep its uncertainty visible, and refuse to overstate.

Panoptes is built around three design commitments. First, evidence before verdict: the interface leads with a plain-language summary and exposes the full statistical drilldown — formulas, units, assumptions, and limitations — one layer down. Second, calibrated combination: heterogeneous signals are combined in the odds domain with an explicit prior drawn from a declared calibration cohort, so that the strength of evidence is separable from base rates. Third, verifiable practice: the project ships a canonical baseline prompt set and a public, hash-chained catalog so that claims about model behavior can be checked, replicated, and extended by anyone without trusting the project with raw outputs.

2. Related work

Watermarking. Kirchenbauer et al. introduced the green-list watermark for large language models, in which a pseudorandom partition of the vocabulary biases token selection and detection reduces to a binomial test [1]. Aaronson and Kirchner developed a related cryptographic scheme [2]. These tests are powerful when the text is unmodified and the scheme is known, and degrade gracefully under editing — behavior we quantify explicitly through effect sizes, power estimates, and a dilution statistic rather than a bare p-value.

Provenance. Content credentials and signed manifests (e.g., C2PA) attest the origin of a file rather than the statistical character of its text [3]. Panoptes treats provenance as an independent evidence channel: when present and verified it dominates statistical inference; when absent it says nothing, and the interface says so.

Detection and calibration. Detector scores are known to be miscalibrated under domain shift, short inputs, and paraphrase. Panoptes therefore reports an explicit reliability error for the detector on its calibration cohort and penalizes conclusions on short or unsupported inputs instead of emitting an unhedged number.

Verifiable artifacts. Our community catalog draws on the same principle as transparency logs and timestamping services: commit to content with cryptographic hashes, anchor the commitment publicly, and let anyone verify. OpenTimestamps provides a pragmatic anchor to the Bitcoin blockchain without any custom chain [4].

3. System and methods

3.1 Segmentation and evidence state

Input text (or an uploaded document) is segmented into analysis units, and each unit is scored independently before combination. The pipeline maintains an explicit evidence state: when the usable token count falls below a supported threshold (50 tokens), the system reports that evidence is insufficient rather than emitting a number that would invite overconfidence. This refusal path is a first-class result, not an error.

3.2 Calibrated posterior

Detector evidence is combined in the odds domain. Given declared prior odds O0 — an explicit analysis parameter, even odds by default, labeled with the calibration cohort it was drawn from — and a likelihood ratio LR aggregated across segments, the posterior odds and probability are

O1 = O0 × LR,   P = O1 / (1 + O1) (1)

Reporting the decomposition — prior odds, likelihood ratio, posterior odds — separates “what the tests say” from “what we believed before testing,” and the interface presents exactly these quantities. The detector’s expected calibration error on the cohort is reported alongside as the reliability error, so that a confident posterior from a poorly calibrated detector is visibly discounted.

3.3 Watermark hypothesis tests

For each configured public watermark scheme, eligible tokens are counted and the number falling in the scheme’s green set, G, is compared against the chance expectation under the null. With green-list fraction γ and n eligible tokens,

z = (G − γn) / √(nγ(1 − γ)),   p = 1 − Φ(z) (2)

Across the family of tests run on one document we control the false discovery rate with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, reporting q-values rather than bare p-values [5]. Each test additionally reports the observed green rate with a 95% confidence interval, an effect size (lift over γ), the achieved power of the test at the configured watermark strength and observed n, and a dilution estimate — the fraction of text that would need to carry the watermark to explain the observed lift, which distinguishes “absent” from “edited away.” Tests that cannot run (ineligible text, unknown scheme, insufficient length) report a structured status rather than a silent null.

3.4 Provenance verification

When an upload carries a signed provenance manifest, Panoptes verifies the signature chain and reports the outcome as a separate channel with its own status vocabulary (verified, invalid, not present). Statistical and cryptographic evidence are never blended into a single score; the user sees both, labeled.

3.5 Interface as methodology

The web interface is part of the method. The first view is a plain-language answer with the outcome distribution; the technical laboratory below exposes the posterior decomposition, per-scheme watermark statistics, an evidence contribution waterfall, and the interpretation limits that apply to the specific input. Every statistic carries an inline explanation of what it measures, so the report can be read by the people it affects.

4. Verifiable community baselines

Claims about model behavior should be checkable by anyone, not just by those with API budgets. Panoptes defines a canonical baseline protocol with four properties:

A canonical prompt set. Sixteen prompts — eight prose, eight code — spanning genres, lengths, and task types, versioned as a machine-readable manifest whose own SHA-256 is embedded in the artifact (2092321d…8b06b for v1.0.0). Every run pins this hash, so results are comparable by construction.

Three execution modes. Runs may be produced manually through a chat interface, programmatically through provider APIs, or agent-assisted inside an editor; the interface used is declared in the manifest, because the channel is part of the evidence.

Hash-pinned manifests. A run manifest records the model identity (as reported by the runner), the pinned prompt-set hash, and for each output only its filename, byte count, and SHA-256. Output hashes form a Merkle tree whose root commits to the entire set; the manifest’s own canonical hash identifies the run. Raw outputs never enter the catalog — a validator rejects any manifest that embeds model text, so the public ledger carries evidence, not content.

An append-only public catalog. Submissions append one JSON line to a registry and one manifest file, both validated in continuous integration against JSON Schemas. Optional OpenTimestamps anchoring binds the manifest hash to a Bitcoin block, giving each claim a tamper-evident timestamp [4]. Verification is a single command (baseline.py verify-catalog) and requires no trust in the submitter.

Contribution protocol. A contributor (1) runs the canonical prompt set by any of the three execution modes, (2) finalizes the run, which hashes each output with SHA-256, builds the Merkle root, and self-hashes the canonical manifest, (3) optionally anchors the manifest hash with OpenTimestamps, and (4) opens a pull request containing only the registry line and the manifest file. Raw outputs remain on the contributor’s machine, ignored by version control. External corpora participate by reference rather than by inclusion: a signed pointer manifest records the source URL, access conditions, download instructions, and a SHA-256 integrity commitment, so third-party data is never copied into the repository.

Relationship to the analyzer. The catalog is deliberately not a runtime input to the analyzer. In the current release, source-family attribution is a hand-tuned stylometric softmax over surface features, and the posterior’s prior odds are supplied per request; no baseline output influences a live score. The reference corpus exists so that future calibrated components — fitted family centroids, per-model detector reliability estimates, human-control comparisons — can be trained and audited against material whose provenance is publicly verifiable, and so that claims about model behavior can be replicated independently.

6. Limitations

Text-only attribution is fundamentally bounded. Detector likelihood ratios inherit the calibration and bias of the underlying models and degrade under paraphrase, translation, and heavy editing; the reliability error is reported precisely so that this degradation is visible. Watermark tests cover only known, configured schemes and only text that survived editing; a negative result under low power is weak evidence, and the interface says so. Provenance covers only files that carry credentials. In agent-assisted baseline runs, the model identity is self-reported by the runner; the catalog attests what was claimed and what resulted, not the ground truth of the serving stack. Finally, the reference set is small and English-heavy; we treat it as calibration infrastructure, not as a benchmark of model quality.

7. Ethics and responsible use

Panoptes is designed against the two failure modes that harm people: accusation on thin evidence, and false reassurance from a negative test. The system surfaces evidence state, sample size, power, and interpretation limits on every report, and its documentation prohibits using a single score as sole grounds for consequential action. Community submissions carry hashes only, so contributing evidence never requires disclosing model outputs or personal text.

8. Conclusion

Panoptes reframes AI-text attribution as the presentation of calibrated, inspectable evidence rather than the emission of a verdict, and pairs the analyzer with a verification-first community protocol for baseline data. Both are open, local-first, and built to be checked. We invite replication: run the canonical prompts, hash your outputs, and append your evidence to the catalog.
